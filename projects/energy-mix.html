Optimizing the U.S. Energy Mix to Reduce Greenhouse Gas Emissions by 2030

Python • Ridge Regression • Linear Programming

An applied mathematics capstone examining the relationship between U.S. energy-source composition and greenhouse gas emissions, followed by optimization of a lower-emission energy mix for 2030.

Project Overview

This project investigates how changes in the composition of the U.S. energy mix are associated with greenhouse gas emission intensity. It then uses mathematical optimization to identify potential energy mixes that could reduce emissions by 2030 while remaining within practical generation constraints.

The project combines two models. Ridge regression evaluates historical relationships between energy-source shares and emission intensity, while linear programming determines an energy mix that minimizes projected lifecycle emissions.

Data Sources

The historical analysis combines U.S. energy-mix data from Our World in Data with greenhouse gas emissions data from Climate Watch for 1965 through 2023. The optimization model also uses projected 2030 energy demand from the U.S. Energy Information Administration's Annual Energy Outlook 2025 and published lifecycle-emission estimates for individual energy sources.

Data Preparation and Methods

The historical datasets were cleaned, standardized, and merged by year. Energy consumption values were converted into percentage shares, and greenhouse gas emission intensity was calculated in metric tons of CO₂-equivalent per terawatt-hour.

A multiple linear regression model was initially considered, but strong multicollinearity among energy-source shares produced unstable and unrealistic coefficients. Ridge regression was therefore used to stabilize the model while retaining seven energy-source predictors.

The second part of the project used linear programming to minimize projected 2030 lifecycle emissions. The model incorporated a projected energy demand of 26,921 TWh, energy-source bounds, a minimum baseload requirement, limits on fossil-fuel dependence, and restrictions on the combined share of solar and wind. Additional scenarios examined carbon capture and expanded energy-storage capacity.

Analysis and Key Findings

The Ridge model used an optimized regularization parameter of approximately 0.003678 and explained about 98.8% of the historical variation in emission intensity. The analysis indicated that higher shares of lower-emission sources were generally associated with reduced emission intensity, while larger fossil-fuel shares were associated with increased intensity.

The linear-programming model tested whether the United States could reduce emissions by at least 50% from the 2005 baseline while satisfying the generation constraints. Scenarios without carbon capture could not reach the target under the established limits, while carbon-capture scenarios produced feasible lower-emission energy mixes.

  • The Ridge model achieved an R² of approximately 0.988, demonstrating a strong fit to the historical data.
  • The constrained optimization model could not meet the emissions target without carbon capture.
  • Carbon-capture scenarios produced projected emissions between approximately 2.53 and 2.68 billion metric tons of CO₂-equivalent.
  • In the final carbon-capture scenario, the optimized mix included 13% solar, 12% wind, 15% nuclear, 32.95% natural gas, 20% oil, no coal, 7% other renewables, and a small hydropower share.
  • Expanded storage capacity allowed solar generation to increase to 20% while reducing the model's reliance on natural gas.
